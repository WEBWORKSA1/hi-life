Compound interest explained

Compound interest means you earn returns on your original money and on the returns it has already earned. Early on, growth looks slow. Over decades, the interest you earn each year can exceed what you deposit — the bend in the chart above.

The Rule of 72

Divide 72 by your yearly return to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 6% it doubles about every 12 years; at 9%, every 8 years. The calculator shows the exact figure for your inputs.

What to do with this

  • Start now. Time in the market does more work than the amount you start with.
  • Automate deposits. Monthly contributions smooth out market ups and downs (dollar-cost averaging).
  • Watch fees and taxes. Use tax-advantaged accounts where available and low-cost funds.

Savings accounts pay a fixed rate; investments like index funds have higher expected returns but can fall in value. The calculator assumes a steady rate for clarity.

Frequently asked questions

What is a realistic return?

High-yield savings accounts pay a fixed rate set by the bank. Diversified stock index funds have historically averaged higher long-run returns but with ups and downs year to year.

Does compounding frequency matter much?

A little. Monthly vs yearly compounding makes a small difference; the rate and the number of years matter far more.

Is the result before or after inflation?

Before. Subtract expected inflation from your rate to see the result in today's money.
