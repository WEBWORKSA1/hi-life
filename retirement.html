How this retirement calculator works

The calculator grows your current savings and monthly contributions at your expected return each year until your retirement age. In parallel it inflates your desired yearly spending and divides it by your withdrawal rate to find the nest egg you would need on the day you retire. The difference between the two is your shortfall or surplus.

Your FIRE number ("financial independence, retire early") is simply yearly spending divided by your withdrawal rate. At 4%, that is 25 times your spending. Once your invested savings reach it, work becomes optional.

The 4% rule, in plain English

The 4% rule comes from historical studies of US stock and bond returns: a retiree who withdrew 4% of their starting balance, then raised the amount with inflation, rarely ran out of money over 30 years. For longer retirements (early retirees) or if you want more safety, 3.25–3.5% is more conservative. Use the slider to see how much it changes your target.

Three levers that matter most

  1. Savings rate. Raising what you save each month has a bigger effect than chasing higher returns.
  2. Time. Every extra year adds a contribution, a year of growth, and one fewer year of withdrawals.
  3. Costs. Low-cost index funds keep more of the return in your pocket. A 1% annual fee can consume roughly a quarter of the final balance over a long career.

Projections assume a steady return each year. Real markets rise and fall, so treat the result as a planning range, not a promise, and revisit it once a year.

Frequently asked questions

What return should I assume?

A balanced, diversified portfolio has historically returned roughly 5–7% a year before inflation over long periods. Use a lower number to be conservative.

Does this include government pensions or Social Security?

No. Subtract any expected pension income from your yearly spending figure to see how much your own savings must cover.

Is the 4% rule still safe?

It is a starting point, not a guarantee. Many planners suggest 3.5–4% for a 30-year retirement and lower rates for retirements of 40+ years.
