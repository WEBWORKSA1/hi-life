The math in one paragraph

Time to financial independence depends mostly on one number: the share of your take-home pay you save. Saving more does two jobs at once. It grows your investments faster, and it proves you can live on less, which lowers the amount you need. That is why the relationship is a curve, not a straight line: going from 10% to 25% can cut decades, while going from 60% to 70% cuts a few years.

Rough benchmarks

Starting from zero with about a 5% real return and a 4% withdrawal rate, the classic illustration popularised by early-retirement writers is roughly 51 years of work at a 10% savings rate, about 16–17 years at 50%, and around a decade at 65%. Your own numbers above include what you have already invested.

How to raise your savings rate

  • Cut the big three: housing, transport and food usually beat dozens of small cuts.
  • Save raises: bank at least half of every pay increase before lifestyle catches up.
  • Automate it: move savings out on payday so you never see it.
  • Grow income: skills and job moves raise the ceiling on what you can save.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as savings rate?

Money you invest or use to pay down debt beyond the minimum, divided by your take-home pay. Include retirement contributions taken from your paycheck in both numbers.

Why use a real return?

A real (after-inflation) return keeps everything in today’s money, so the target and your spending stay comparable.

Is 4% safe for early retirement?

For very long retirements many planners prefer 3.25–3.5%. Move the slider to see how much later that makes your date.
